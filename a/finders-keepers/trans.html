1Finders, keepers? Not in Tokyo

2In one of the world's busiest cities, a forgotten umbrella sets off a small civic drama.

3The system that handles it says a lot about trust.

4Every weekday morning, a small miracle goes largely unnoticed in Tokyo.

5A commuter slumps onto a train, dozes off, and wakes up one stop too late, minus her umbrella, her scarf or, in the more spectacular cases, an envelope stuffed with cash.

6By the time she has finished cursing, the item has often begun an improbable journey.

7It goes from a station attendant's desk to a neighbourhood police box and, if nobody comes forward, to a central lost-property office, where it is logged, labelled and stored.

8The scale is staggering.

9Police in the capital handle several million lost items a year.

10By sheer volume, umbrellas lead the pack, yet almost nobody comes to claim them; a cheap umbrella is the one possession Tokyoites seem happy to lose.

11Wallets are another story.

12A striking share of them find their way back to their owners, cash and all.

13Economists would start with incentives.

14Japanese law obliges finders to hand in what they pick up, and entitles them to a reward of between 5% and 20% of the item's value, payable by the owner.

15In practice, many finders reportedly waive it.

16Incentives, then, explain only part of the story.

17The rest lies in infrastructure and habit.

18The koban, the small police boxes found in nearly every neighbourhood, make doing the right thing cheap: there is almost always one within a short walk.

19The process itself is gloriously dull, a form, a receipt, a database entry.

20Social scientists might call this lowering the cost of honesty.

21It is a lesson that travels.

22A much-cited experiment published in Science in 2019 left thousands of "lost" wallets at hotels, banks and offices in 40 countries.

23Counterintuitively, wallets containing more money were more likely to be returned, not less.

24The researchers' explanation was that people dislike seeing themselves as thieves, and that this feeling grows with the size of the temptation.

25Honesty, in other words, is not merely a personal virtue.

26It is also a service that a society either provides or fails to provide.

27When the cost of doing the right thing falls to nearly zero, most people do it; when it does not, no amount of moral exhortation will fill the gap.

28Meanwhile, the umbrellas pile up in Iidabashi, a quiet monument to the one thing Tokyo cannot legislate: the rain.
