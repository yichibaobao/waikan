参考答案（英文原文）

1Long live cash: why Japan is in no hurry to go cashless

2In much of the rich world, tapping a phone has replaced fumbling for coins.

3In Japan the old habit survives for reasons that are more rational than they look.

4Stand in the queue at a Tokyo bakery at lunchtime and watch the wallets come out.

5Some customers wave a phone at the reader; a surprising number still count out coins with the unhurried air of people who have nowhere to be.

6To a visitor from Shenzhen or Stockholm, where cash has become a rarity, the scene looks like a time capsule.

7Japan's officials see it differently, and have spent years trying to coax shoppers into going cashless.

8The first reason is that cash works remarkably well here.

9Crime is low, so carrying a thick wad of notes feels safe, and cash machines are everywhere, including in almost every convenience store.

10Cash also keeps working when the power fails, which counts for something in a country that lives with earthquakes.

11Older customers, who make up a growing share of the population, tend to trust what they can hold in their hands.

12For them, and for plenty of younger people, the old system is not broken, so there is little to fix.

13The second reason is that the bill for convenience lands on someone else.

14Shoppers pay nothing extra to use cash, whereas shops hand over a slice of every card sale to the firms that run the payment networks.

15For a small bakery working on thin margins, that slice can be the difference between a profit and a loss.

16Many owners therefore accept cards grudgingly, and a few quietly discourage them.

17Officials have long tried to nudge people along.

18A few years ago the government dangled rebates in front of anyone who paid by card or phone, and the share of cashless payments crept upward.

19But incentives of that kind tend to fade once the money runs out.

20What may finally tip the balance is not persuasion but arithmetic.

21Japan's workforce is shrinking, and every hour a shopkeeper spends counting the till costs more each year.

22Handling cash is a hidden expense: notes must be counted, banked and ferried about in armoured vans, and someone has to keep all those machines topped up.

23Shops and banks are beginning to realise that the cheapest way to take money is the one with the fewest humans involved.

24None of this makes Japan's attachment to cash irrational.

25People stick with what works, and the cost of switching is easy to overlook for those who stand to gain from it.

26Habits rarely give way because someone has made a persuasive case; they do so when the old way becomes more expensive for those who set the price.

27Until then, the bakery queue will remain a quiet standoff between two ways of paying.
