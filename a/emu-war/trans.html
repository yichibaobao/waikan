1Outgunned by feathers: the war Australia lost to a flightless bird

2In 1932 soldiers armed with machine guns set out to rid farmland of emus.

3The birds, it turned out, had other plans.

4After the First World War, thousands of former soldiers were given land in Western Australia and encouraged to grow wheat.

5Times were hard: prices fell during the Depression, and the government's promised subsidies never materialised.

6Then the emus arrived.

7Some 20,000 of them, migrating inland after the breeding season, found the farms irresistible: open water, cleared land and endless rows of crops.

8They trampled fences, which let rabbits in, and devoured what was left.

9A small military unit was dispatched, led by a major, with two machine guns and thousands of rounds of ammunition.

10On paper, it looked like a mismatch.

11And so it proved, though not in the way anyone expected.

12The emus scattered the moment the guns opened fire, splitting into small groups that were maddeningly hard to hit.

13One carefully planned ambush ended when a gun jammed with only a dozen birds down.

14Mounting a gun on a truck did not help either: the birds easily outran it over the bumpy ground, and the gunner could barely aim.

15After about a week the soldiers were withdrawn, amid mockery in the newspapers and awkward questions in parliament.

16They returned soon afterwards at the farmers' request and claimed nearly a thousand kills, though the figures were disputed.

17Even by generous estimates, each dead bird had cost around ten bullets.

18The officer in charge reportedly admired the emus' toughness, likening them to tanks.

19The episode, now known as the Great Emu War, survives mostly as a joke.

20Yet it carries a serious lesson about pitting brute force against a problem that is really about ecology.

21In the end, what worked better was a bounty system and sturdier fences.

22Emus remain plentiful, and still wander onto farms from time to time.

23Australia, meanwhile, has learnt to laugh about the only war it is said to have lost to a bird.

24The emus, characteristically, have declined to comment.
