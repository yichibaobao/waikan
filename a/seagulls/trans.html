1Eyes on the chips: the seaside war between humans and gulls

2Across the world's beach towns, bold seabirds have learned that tourists are an easy meal.

3Scientists think they have found a surprisingly simple defence.

4It happens in a flash.

5A family sits down on a sea wall in Cornwall, unwraps a paper parcel of fish and chips, and before anyone has taken a bite, a herring gull swoops down and makes off with the biggest piece.

6The children shriek, the parents shout, and the bird retreats to a rooftop to enjoy its prize.

7Similar scenes play out every summer from Brighton to Sydney to Cape Town.

8Gulls were not always this cheeky.

9For most of their history they fed at sea, scavenging behind fishing boats and picking through the shoreline.

10But as fish stocks dwindled and towns filled up with litter, rubbish bins and outdoor diners, many birds moved inland and adapted with remarkable speed.

11A city gull can live for 20 years, remembers where food is easy to come by, and learns by watching other gulls get away with theft.

12Residents are at their wits' end.

13Councils have tried everything from spiked roofs and fake owls to fines for anyone caught feeding the birds.

14Some towns have hired falconers, whose hawks patrol the beach to scare gulls off.

15Most of these measures work for a while; then the gulls catch on and come back.

16Researchers studying gull behaviour have come up with a cheaper idea: stare them down.

17In one experiment, scientists placed a bag of chips on the ground and watched how long passing gulls took to approach it.

18When a person kept their eyes fixed on the bird, it hesitated far longer than when the person looked away, and many gave up altogether.

19Gulls, it seems, are wary of being watched, perhaps because a staring animal is more likely to fight back.

20There is a catch, of course.

21Only a minority of the gulls dared to come near the food, and the boldest barely paused before grabbing it.

22And it is hard to keep an eye on a gull while also eating, chatting and keeping track of small children.

23Still, the finding hints at something that frustrated towns often miss.

24Gulls are not villains; they are opportunists doing what any sensible animal would do when food is left lying around.

25Many species are in decline at sea even as their urban cousins thrive, which makes culling them hard to justify.

26The best defence may simply be to eat indoors, keep bins closed and, when all else fails, look the bird in the eye.

27It will not win the war, but it might save your lunch.
