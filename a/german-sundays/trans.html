1Hush, it's Sunday: why Germans happily let their shops stay shut

2In a country famous for efficiency, one day a week is set aside for doing almost nothing at all.

3Newcomers grumble; most Germans would not have it any other way.

4Arrive in a German town on a Sunday and you may think something has gone badly wrong.

5Supermarkets are dark, department stores are locked, and the high street is eerily quiet.

6This is not a strike or a holiday; it is simply the law.

7Sunday trading is tightly restricted, and the constitution itself protects the day as one of rest and spiritual uplift.

8A few exceptions keep the country running.

9Bakeries may open for a few hours, so that nobody is deprived of fresh rolls, and shops in railway stations do a roaring trade.

10Otherwise, anyone who forgot to buy milk on Saturday must wait, or knock on a neighbour's door.

11The quiet extends well beyond the shops.

12In many places it is frowned upon, and sometimes against local rules, to mow the lawn, drill holes in walls or even drop bottles into a recycling bin on a Sunday.

13The clatter of glass, the logic goes, should not disturb anyone's rest.

14Neighbours have been known to complain about a washing machine left spinning.

15Business groups have long argued that the rules cost money and hurt retailers competing with online shops.

16Each attempt to loosen them meets fierce resistance from an unlikely alliance of churches and trade unions.

17Churches defend Sunday as sacred; unions defend it as the one day shop workers can count on spending with their families.

18Courts have mostly sided with them.

19Many foreigners find the arrangement maddening at first and then, rather to their surprise, come to love it.

20With nothing to buy and nowhere to queue, people walk, cycle, visit relatives or linger over long lunches.

21Parks fill up; phones go quiet.

22It is a weekly reminder that not every hour needs to be productive.

23And on Monday morning, the shops open again, as punctual as ever.
