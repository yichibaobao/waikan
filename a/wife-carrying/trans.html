1For better or for worse: the race where Finnish men carry their wives

2Every summer, a sleepy town in central Finland plays host to one of the world's oddest sporting events.

3The rules are simple, the prize is beer, and nobody takes it entirely seriously.

4Sonkajärvi, a town of a few thousand people, is not an obvious place to stage a world championship.

5Yet each July thousands of visitors descend on it to watch men sprint through mud with a woman slung over their shoulders.

6The sport, known in Finnish as eukonkanto, has been held there since 1992.

7Its origins, supposedly, lie in the 19th century.

8Local folklore tells of a robber called Herkko Rosvo-Ronkainen, who is said to have tested would-be members of his gang by making them carry heavy sacks through the forest.

9Darker versions of the tale have his men raiding nearby villages and running off with women.

10Whether any of this happened is anyone's guess; the town is happy to cash in on the legend either way.

11Historians may scoff, but a good story has never hurt tourism.

12The course is 253.5 metres long and features two dry obstacles and a water pit about a metre deep.

13The "wife" does not have to be married to the carrier, or, indeed, to anyone.

14She must, however, be over 17 and weigh at least 49kg; anyone lighter is weighed down with a rucksack of ballast to make up the difference.

15Most teams opt for the so-called Estonian carry, in which the wife hangs upside down with her legs around the man's neck and her arms clasped around his waist.

16It looks undignified, but it frees up the carrier's hands and keeps the weight close to his back.

17Estonian couples were so good at it that they dominated the event for much of the 2000s.

18The winners take home the wife's weight in beer, which makes a heavier partner a burden on the course but a bonus on the podium.

19Many entrants turn up in fancy dress, cheer each other on and fall into the water pit with gusto.

20Spectators lap it up.

21Yet the top contenders train for months, because shaving a second off their time can mean the difference between glory and a long, wet walk home.

22The idea has spread: similar races are now held in America and Britain, among other places.

23Some purists complain that the spin-offs miss the point.

24The point, the organisers insist, is not really about winning at all; it is about letting your hair down in a country often stereotyped as quiet and reserved.

25And for the couples who make it to the finish, there is a lesson worth remembering: marriage, like the course, goes better when you pull together and keep your footing in the mud.
