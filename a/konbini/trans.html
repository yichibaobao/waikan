1Always open, never enough hands: the strain on Japan's convenience stores

2Japan's corner shops pay bills, post parcels and print documents at three in the morning.

3Keeping them that way is getting harder as the people who staff them grow scarce.

4It is two o'clock in the morning in a quiet suburb of Osaka, and the only lights on the street belong to a convenience store.

5Inside, a lone clerk is restocking rice balls, while a taxi driver warms up a bento in the microwave and a student pays her phone bill at the till.

6Nobody seems to find any of this remarkable.

7Japan has more than 55,000 of these shops, known locally as konbini, or roughly one for every 2,200 people.

8They began as copies of American corner stores in the 1970s, but have since grown into something closer to a public utility.

9Customers can pay taxes and utility bills, send and collect parcels, buy concert tickets, withdraw cash and print official documents such as residence certificates.

10After earthquakes, the chains are among the first to reopen, and local governments count on them to keep food and water flowing.

11All of this rests on a business model that leaves very little room for error.

12Most stores are run by franchise owners, who pay the parent company a hefty share of their gross profit in return for its brand, its supply chain and its data.

13Lorries deliver fresh food several times a day, so shelves rarely sit empty and little is left to go to waste.

14The system is famously efficient, but it depends on someone being behind the counter around the clock.

15That someone is increasingly hard to find.

16Japan's working-age population has been shrinking for three decades, and part-time jobs that pay little and involve night shifts are the first to go unfilled.

17Many owners now cover the gaps themselves, working 12-hour shifts for weeks on end.

18A few years ago, an owner who shut his shop overnight without permission, saying he could no longer stay awake, briefly became a national talking point.

19The chains have responded in several ways.

20Foreign workers, many of them students from Vietnam, Nepal and China, now make up a large share of staff in big cities.

21Self-checkout machines are spreading, and some stores have experimented with closing at night or running without staff at all.

22Yet every shortcut chips away at what made the konbini indispensable in the first place: a real person, ready to help, at any hour.

23There is a lesson here for other rich countries that are ageing fast.

24Services that people take for granted are often propped up by cheap, flexible labour that is quietly disappearing.

25Japan's convenience stores may be the first place where the shortfall shows up, but they are unlikely to be the last.

26Sooner or later, customers may have to accept that convenience, like everything else, has a price.
