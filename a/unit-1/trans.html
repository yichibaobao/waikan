1From scratch, every morning: the quiet arithmetic of a small-town bakery

2In an ageing town where shops sit empty, one family bakery has survived for 60 years.

3How long it can keep going is another story.

4Most mornings, Kenji Sato is at work by four, long before the first commuters slump onto the early train.

5He makes everything from flour, butter and patience, and he is in no hurry to change.

6His grandfather opened the shop in 1964, and the oven, the scales and the wooden till have stayed where they were ever since.

7Walking in feels like stepping into a time capsule.

8On Saturdays customers queue out of the door, and a surprising number of them are young people from the city with nowhere to be.

9The bakery is famously cash-only.

10Its prices are gloriously dull: a loaf has cost the same for a decade.

11Regulars count out coins at the counter, while visitors fumble for their wallets and look around for a card reader that is not there.

12Mr Sato sees it differently from his younger customers: card firms take a slice of every sale, and he already lives on thin margins.

13Cash, he argues, is not broken, so there is no reason to fix it.

14Counterintuitively, refusing cards may even save him money.

15Whereas a card payment costs him a fee, a coin dropped into the till costs him nothing extra.

16The real strain on the business is not money but people.

17Like the rest of Japan, the town's working-age population is shrinking.

18Bakers willing to start at four in the morning have grown scarce, and the job is among the first to go unfilled.

19For weeks on end last winter, Mr Sato and his wife covered the gaps themselves, working almost around the clock.

20His son, who has long lived in Osaka, has grudgingly offered to help at weekends.

21Mr Sato does not take that for granted.

22A consultant from the city once tried to coax him into opening a second shop, with a card reader, self-checkout and a website.

23The incentive was clear: more customers and a hefty share of the tourist trade.

24Mr Sato listened politely, handed over a free loaf, and said no.

25His reasoning was more rational than it looked.

26A bigger business would rest on borrowed money and leave little room for error; a small one can live with a bad month.

27Every shortcut, he believes, would chip away at what made the shop indispensable in the first place.

28Over the decades, the bakery has grown into something like a public utility.

29The town has come to count on it, largely because it is always open.

30Neighbours leave parcels there for each other to collect, and anyone who finds a lost possession hands it in at the counter.

31Reportedly, the shop has returned more lost wallets than the local police box.

32In practice, it works as a small civic centre.

33Its virtue lies in the people who stop by, not merely in what they buy.

34No amount of clever marketing will replace that.

35Still, rising costs may soon tip the balance.

36Flour and electricity prices have crept upward for three years, and the savings that propped up the shop through bad times are running out.

37Sooner or later, Mr Sato will have to decide whether the bakery stands to gain more from changing than from staying the same.

38For now, he would rather stick with what he knows than give way.

39By the time the shop closes each afternoon, almost nothing has gone to waste.

40It is, in its own way, a lesson that travels.
